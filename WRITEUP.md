# Ablak: a counter-side guide for Hungary

**Hiya Voice AI Challenge · Real-Time Assistance · Alina Erkulova**
Live: https://alinaerkul.github.io/ablak/ · Code: https://github.com/alinaerkul/ablak

**In one sentence:** a browser voice assistant that explains what a Hungarian clerk just asked, in English or Russian, tells you which document to hand over, and says so when it is not sure instead of guessing.

## The problem

I needed a dentist. At a state hospital I tried to book one with my TAJ card, but the receptionist spoke only Hungarian and a translation app did not help. I understood “third floor, one o’clock”, went up, waited an hour, never got the appointment, and left for work. In the end I waited for a trip to Kyrgyzstan and saw a dentist there.

It is not only me: I am in several Facebook groups for international people in Hungary, and every week I see a few posts asking for someone who speaks Hungarian and English to come along to a hospital, a bank or a public office. People ask strangers for this because nothing else helps in the moment at the counter.

## What I built and who it is for

**Ablak** listens to the Hungarian spoken at an immigration office, bank, clinic, supermarket or phone shop and tells you, in English or Russian, what is being asked, what to do right now, and **which document to take out of the folder**. It then speaks a Hungarian reply you can repeat. It is for international students and newcomers who have shop-level Hungarian and none for an official counter. I am one of them (Bishkek to Budapest, Stipendium Hungaricum), and I also run **Jany Jashoo**, an information platform for Central Asian students arriving in Hungary, which Ablak could become part of.

Around the core there are four more tools: **say it in Hungarian** (speak English or Russian, see the matching prepared Hungarian phrase, tap to speak it), a **practice mode** where the app plays the clerk in Hungarian, a **courier card** with your address and fixed phrases, and a **call simulator** that shows how an agent could phone on your behalf (simulated; no real call is made).

## Why voice, and not a keyboard

Both hands hold documents and the clerk is waiting, so there is no time to type. And you cannot type a word you did not catch: Hungarian adds endings, so *lakcímkártya* (address card) is heard as *lakcímkártyáját*, which a newcomer cannot spell by ear. Where there is time and you know the words (practice, the courier card), typing works too.

## What it does differently

**It refuses when it is not sure.** Clerks often try to help, but a confident wrong answer gets in the way: the clerk thinks you understood. So the core is not a model call but a written, reviewable set of 237 counter phrases in 840 spoken forms. Matching combines character 4-gram similarity, which survives Hungarian suffixes and recognition noise, with IDF-weighted keyword stems. The result is one of three visibly different outcomes: high confidence, check this, or not understood. An optional model fallback exists for phrases outside the set: off by default, labelled unverified, and never allowed to name a document. The simulated agent says it is an AI and never gives identity or health numbers.

It needs no key, account or backend. 159 automated checks run in the browser, and a second suite drives the real page in Chrome. They have caught real bugs, including a stem collision that would have told a user to hand over a physical card instead of answering a question; see `ai.md`.

## How I would measure success

Not measured yet; this is what I would track with real users. **Correct answers:** the share of real counter phrases the app explains correctly. **Safe refusals:** the share of wrong matches it refuses instead of showing; a confidently wrong answer is the failure that matters most. **Independence:** whether a newcomer gets through a visit without asking a stranger to come along.

## Limits and what is out of scope

All 237 phrases were generated by an AI assistant; I reviewed their content, and a native Hungarian speaker has not yet reviewed the wording. Chrome's speech service (Google's) does the transcription, and Hungarian recognition degrades in noisy rooms; an optional Whisper engine (own free Groq key) is there for that case. The confidence thresholds are fitted to my own test phrases, not to real logged speech. Out of scope for now: translating free conversation, filling in forms, and making real phone calls.
